# 🧭 Key Aspects to Check Before Accepting a “Data-Driven” Conclusion

I came to know about Darrell Huff’s *How to Lie with Statistics* as part of my PG Diploma in Applied Statistics. While statistics serve as a useful and powerful way to summarize vast data, they can also be misleading if not carefully examined. 
The book highlights several key aspects to keep in mind before accepting any “data-driven” conclusion:

---

## 1. The Sample — Who was counted?

**Question:** Is the sample representative of the group we’re talking about?  
**Why it matters:** Biased or small samples distort results.  
**Example:** A survey about national income based on readers of a financial magazine will overestimate wealth.  

---

## 2. The Average — Which “average” do they mean?

**Question:** Are they using the mean, median, or mode?  
**Why it matters:** Each tells a different story, especially when the data are skewed.  
**Example:** “Average income is $100k” could mean a few high earners have pulled up the mean.  

---

## 3. The Graph — How is it drawn?

**Question:** Are scales honest? Do the axes start at zero?  
**Why it matters:** Visual distortions can exaggerate small changes.  
**Example:** A bar chart starting its y-axis at 90 instead of 0 makes a minor difference look dramatic.  

---

## 4. The Missing Context — What’s not being said?

**Question:** What time period or comparison has been chosen? What data were omitted?  
**Why it matters:** Selective time frames or missing information can completely reverse a conclusion.  
**Example:** Showing profits for one lucky quarter may hide a longer-term decline.  

---

## 5. The Percentages — Percent of what?

**Question:** Are the percentages based on absolute numbers?  
**Why it matters:** “50% increase” means little if the base number is tiny.  
**Example:** Cases rising from 2 to 3 represent a 50% increase, but only one additional case.  

---

## 6. The Cause and Effect — Is it correlation or causation?

**Question:** Does the data actually prove that one thing caused another?  
**Why it matters:** Correlation alone can mislead decision-making.  
**Example:** People wear sunglasses and drink more water in summer—but one does not cause the other.  

---

## 7. The Post Hoc Fallacy — Are we jumping to conclusions?

**Question:** Is the data interpreted to fit a preconceived story?  
**Why it matters:** People often search for evidence to confirm existing beliefs rather than test them objectively.  

---

## 8. The “Gee-Whiz” Graphs and Half-Truths

**Question:** Is the presentation polished but shallow?  
**Why it matters:** Fancy visuals or jargon can disguise weak reasoning.  
**Example:** “Risk reduced by 50%” sounds impressive—but that may mean a drop from 2% to 1%.  

---

## 9. The Probable Error — What’s the margin of error or uncertainty?

**Question:** How precise are these numbers?  
**Why it matters:** Every measurement has noise; ignoring uncertainty gives a false sense of accuracy.  

---

## 10. The Hidden Agenda — Who benefits from the conclusion?

**Question:** Who collected, analyzed, or funded the data?  
**Why it matters:** Conflicts of interest often shape what is emphasized or left out of reports.
